# Equal playing time in rec league basketball

Equal playing time means every player gets the same share of the minutes. It doesn't mean everyone plays the same quarters, or that everyone's on the court together for the same stretches. It's about the total.

Most rec leagues expect it, especially in the younger age groups. Kids sign up to play basketball, and parents pay the same fees. Equal minutes is the fairest answer to "how much will my kid play?"

## Minutes, not quarters

A lot of coaches count quarters: everyone plays two, or everyone plays at least one. That's a decent rule of thumb, but it hides big differences. A player who plays two full quarters gets twice the minutes of one who plays half of each.

Counting minutes is more honest. For four 8-minute quarters, the even share is easy to work out: 32 minutes times 5 players on court is 160 player-minutes, shared across your roster.

With 8 or 10 players the numbers come out clean. With 7, 9 or 12 they don't, so a fair plan gets everyone within a minute or so of the even share rather than exactly on it.

## Why it's harder than it sounds

On paper, equal minutes is just math. On game day, three things get in the way.

**The clock doesn't wait for you.** You can only sub at dead balls, and in some stretches there aren't many. A planned sub at 4:00 might happen at 3:20.

**It's hard to keep count.** By the third quarter, most coaches without a chart can't say for sure who's played the most. The kids who've been sitting longest are usually the quiet ones, and they're the easiest to forget.

**The game changes.** Fouls, injuries and late arrivals all upset the numbers, and fixing them while coaching is hard.

None of this is a reason to give up on equal minutes. It's a reason to plan ahead. A printed [rotation chart](/rotation-charts) for your roster size takes care of the first two. The [rotation chart maker](/rotation-chart-maker) does other game lengths, with your players' names on it.

## Equal doesn't mean the same role

Equal minutes doesn't stop you coaching. You still choose who plays together, who brings the ball up and who guards their best player. You still teach, correct and encourage.

What it takes off the table is using minutes as a reward or a punishment. In rec basketball that's usually a good thing. If a player needs a word about effort or behavior, have the conversation. Don't let it show up as a quarter on the bench that nobody explained.

## Talking to parents about playing time

Parents care about playing time more than anything else you do. A short, clear message at the start of the season saves a lot of sideline conversations later.

Something like this works:

> Every player on this team gets equal minutes over the game. I plan the rotation before tip-off so it's fair, and I keep track during the game. Some games won't be exactly even because of fouls or injuries, but it evens out over the season. If you ever have a question about minutes, come and talk to me after the game.

Then do what you said. If a parent does ask, being able to say "she played 17 minutes, same as everyone" ends the conversation in a way that "I think she played a fair bit" doesn't.

## Check the season, not just the game

Even with a good plan, one game might be lopsided. That's fine. What matters is the season.

Keep a running total of each player's minutes. If a player's behind after a few games, because of foul trouble, an early injury or a missed game, give them a bit more in the next few. Sideline Hero keeps that season total for you and shows each player's share of the team's minutes.

## The short version

- Count minutes, not quarters.
- Know each player's even share before tip-off.
- Plan the rotation ahead, and sub at dead balls near the planned times.
- Keep track during the game, and check at halftime.
- Tell parents your approach at the start of the season, then stick to it.
- Look at the season total, not just one game.
