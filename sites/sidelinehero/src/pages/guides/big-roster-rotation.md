# Running a rotation with 10 or more players

A big roster is the hardest to keep fair. With ten or more players, at least half the team is on the bench at any moment, and it only takes a few rushed decisions for some kids to play twice as much as others.

The good news is that big rosters respond well to a plan. Here's how to run one.

## Ten players: two fives

Ten players is the neatest big roster. Everyone's even share of a 32-minute game is exactly 16 minutes, which is two full quarters each.

The simplest plan:

- Players 1 to 5 start and play the first quarter.
- Players 6 to 10 play the second quarter.
- In the second half, mix the groups so each player gets one more quarter and the same five aren't always together.

All your subs happen at quarter breaks, so there's nothing to manage while the clock runs. The [10 player rotation chart](/rotation-charts/10-players) lays it out.

Two things to watch. If your two fives aren't evenly matched, one group can get run off the court. Mix stronger and newer players in each group so both can compete. And swap who starts from game to game, so the second group gets to start sometimes too.

## Eleven and twelve players: smaller, more frequent changes

With eleven or twelve, the numbers stop dividing neatly. The even share for a 32-minute game is 14:33 with eleven and 13:20 with twelve.

Whole-group swaps don't work here, because the groups would be different sizes. The fairest plans change players three or four times a quarter, sometimes one or two, sometimes a whole five. That means more subs, so it pays to have each group ready.

The [11 player](/rotation-charts/11-players) and [12 player](/rotation-charts/12-players) charts show how it works. If your league plays halves, or longer quarters, the [rotation chart maker](/rotation-chart-maker) has those too.

## Have the next group ready

With a big roster you'll sub often, so make each sub quick:

- Tell players at the start of each quarter when they're next in.
- Have the next group waiting at the scorer's table before the dead ball.
- Sub at any dead ball near the planned time. A foul, a timeout or a ball out of bounds all work.

A sub that takes five seconds keeps the game flowing. One that takes thirty, while you find the right kids on a crowded bench, costs you momentum and makes subbing feel like a chore.

## Keep the bench involved

Seven kids sitting is a lot of kids sitting. A bored bench is where frustration starts, and where parents look first.

Give the bench something to do:

- Have them watch one opponent and tell you what they see.
- Have one keep count of the other team's fouls.
- Talk to players as they come off: one thing they did well, one thing to try next time.

Players who feel part of the game while sitting are much happier with the minutes they get.

## Expect the plan to change

With twelve players, one absence changes everyone's minutes. A late arrival, an injury or a foul-out means the remaining time needs sharing again, and with this many players it's very hard to do in your head.

When it happens, work out how much time is left and how far each player is from their share, then close the gaps by the end of the game. Or let the app do it: Sideline Hero re-plans everyone's minutes on the spot when a player fouls out, gets hurt or shows up late.

## Watch the season total

Over a season, small differences add up. A player who's a minute short each game ends up well behind by the end.

Keep a running total, and give a little extra to anyone who's falling behind. Rotating who starts helps too, because starters often pick up a few extra minutes at the beginning of each half.

## The short version

- Ten players: two fives, two quarters each, subs at quarter breaks.
- Eleven or twelve: small changes, several times a quarter.
- Have the next group ready at the table.
- Give the bench a job.
- Re-plan when someone's missing.
- Keep an eye on the season total.
